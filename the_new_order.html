The New Order

If the urge to obey is innate and the master a vacancy, can an order be built that needs no master?

DNA of the Gods · A declared relectura

"The Fear-of-God Cluster" proposed that the inclination to obey is in part heritable. "The Empty Thrones" added that this inclination has no fixed object: the master is a vacancy filled, by turns, by the god, the king, the party, the idol, and today the algorithm. If all of that is true, one last question remains, the most reckless of all: can an order be built that needs no master?

What follows is neither a recipe nor a prophecy. It is an imagined experiment: a coherent architecture, carried to its last consequences, to see whether it holds. I do not claim it would work. I claim it is worth imagining honestly —showing it standing and showing it crack—, because to think a different order is the only way to discover why ours is the way it is.

I. Power as service, not as prize

There is an iron law beneath all political history: in any system where the prize of office is power, office ends up occupied by those who crave power. It is pure adverse selection. However many checks you add, the ambitious will always be hungrier than the upright. The only way for servants to rule rather than masters is for office to stop being a prize.

So imagine that office is not elected: it is offered. Anyone may volunteer. But on accepting it they sign two conditions:

The mortal bond

One: keep the approval of the governed above 50%. Two: if it falls below for a sustained period, accept your own execution. One governs with one's life as the bond.

It may sound brutal, but it is not —or no more than its alternative—. Bad government kills far more people than the single life the ruler stakes: a negligence, a corruption, a decision taken so as not to lose votes can cost hundreds or thousands of lives, and today whoever causes them pays, at most, with losing an election. The new order does only one thing: it makes the scale of responsibility finally match the scale of the harm. It is not cruelty; it is proportion. And beyond that, look at what it does. No one in their right mind wants an office that can kill them; so the mortal stick scares off the self-interested at the door. Only those with a true vocation of service and sacrifice accept it —as the priest or the nun embrace celibacy—: the office self-selects for the virtuous. It is the exact reverse of today.

And it does not reward flattery, as one might fear. In the short term, pandering works; in the long run it is certain death: the populist who dodges hard decisions accumulates problems, and when they burst his approval collapses and he pays with his neck. No one sane governs to scrape past 50%: they aim for the high mark, for a wide cushion, because only a good margin saves them from a single stumble sending them to the scaffold. The system, measured over the long term, forces good government.

The people, moreover, are lenient: they know their ruler is human and that everyone errs. An isolated failure is forgiven; he is even sheltered through a bad patch. What is not tolerated is continued error. Death does not come from being wrong, it comes from not learning. And so execution, when it comes, is doubly the ruler's own: the fruit of his free choice plus his incompetence or his corruption. The governed bear no guilt whatsoever: their vote is one among millions, and the ruler signed the wager knowingly. That absence of guilt is, moreover, what lets the people score honestly —and without honesty, the whole edifice crumbles—.

Such an office is incentivised, of course, with a comfortable life and all expenses paid for the ruler and their partner. Not as bait for the greedy —they wouldn't touch an office that kills with a ten-foot pole— but as support and just reward for the one who risks everything. And it is affordable precisely because they are so few. A double filter, in the end: one must want to serve and know how. A secular priesthood of service, but with a permanent trial by fire: where a cleric's false vocation can hide for a lifetime, here reality examines without respite. And against such a system bribery has no purchase: there are no few pivots to buy, because the decisive power is spread among millions who cannot all be bribed, every month, except by governing with ethics and efficiency.

II. The five powers

A power without checks rots however well it is designed. The new order divides power into five, and the mortal bond rules only where it must.

  • Legislative. The office that sets direction and represents the will of the people. It is the one living under the mortal bond, scored every month. Here power emanates from the people truly and without pause: not every four years, but in a permanent referendum carrying the maximum possible sanction.
  • Executive. In a society without want or enthroned hierarchy, most conflicts dry up on their own, so the executive stays minimal. Its reason for being is to curb the mafias, which are the shadow of the system: the lust for dominion that, exiled from the legitimate paths, takes refuge in the illegal. They live off appetite, not need —drugs, prostitution, gambling, corruption— and so they never vanish. The moral line is clean: total freedom over oneself —even to ruin or drug oneself, if informed—; all the force of the state wherever there is a non-consenting victim. Drugs, in fact, are dispensed free and pure at pharmacies, with exhaustive information: thus the dealer's market is annihilated, while a world that is not hostile strips the drug of its reason for being, which was always to flee.
  • Judicial. The only power that is not subject to popularity, and deliberately so: justice must protect the unpopular, and a judge who needed the majority's approval would hand down lynchings. They are career judges, professional and independent, held to account not for pleasing but for getting it right: three sentences overturned on review, and expulsion from the bench. Expulsion, not death —a judge under threat of death would turn coward—. Thus, safe from the vote, the judiciary is the guardian of minorities against the majority.
  • Economic. The power that historically buys all the others. The new order disarms it at the root, and it deserves its own chapter.
  • Press and media. The most dangerous of all for this system, because the whole of it rests on the people's score reflecting the truth, and the media are precisely the power capable of parting opinion from truth. Diversity and the audience filter credibility —a medium lives off advertising, advertising off the audience, and the audience off credibility—, but that filter yields truth only over a lucid public and with a layer of facts verifiable by all. And here the abyss appears: if that layer of facts is arbitrated by a machine, whoever controls the machine controls the very definition of the real.

III. Property: lifelong usufruct

Here is the material foundation of all the rest. Perpetual private property is abolished: property belongs to all the people, and each person enjoys in life the full usufruct of whatever they accumulate. The incentive to produce and prosper stays intact —for decades you live off your effort— but at your death everything reverts to the commons. There is no inheritance.

That does not condemn each generation to start from zero, but from equality. What reverts is split in two halves: one sustains the commons —communal buildings with roof and food for all who need them, spartan on purpose, and with the right to denounce their shortcomings—; the other is distributed as endowments, as many as there are people coming of age, appraised at equal value and allotted by lot, so that all begin adult life from the same starting line.

Dynasties, quite simply, cannot compound. No one knows the day of their death, and whoever amassed a fortune did not do so by giving it away: their own hoarding nature keeps them from parting with it in time, so they die with their hands full —just when everything reverts—. Death is the great equaliser, and greed its unwitting accomplice.

Turning usufruct into money

And what if someone wishes, in life, to turn their usufruct into money? Then they cede it to the state, which auctions it. And the state keeps its share: 50% on real estate and 10% on securities and assets of any other kind (the seller keeps the remaining 50% / 90%). Housing —a scarce, fixed good, the root of everyone's shelter— is thus recycled through the commons and kept accessible, taxing its hoarding heavily; productive capital is barely taxed, so as not to choke enterprise. Every transaction feeds the commons, and liquidating to found a dynasty becomes, in passing, a terrible deal.

One last objection remains, that of comfort: if the safety net were comfortable, no one would move and society would stall. That is why the floor is dignity, never comfort: a communal dormitory and canteen save you from dying, but they are spartan. That distance between the floor and the good life is the incentive. And the entrepreneur does not vanish: he builds for his own life —which he enjoys in full—, for the honour and the name that do endure beyond death, and with a starting endowment that everyone receives, which democratises enterprise instead of reserving it for heirs.

IV. The two keys

This whole edifice hangs, in the end, on two pieces. The first is that spartan floor. The second is a lucid people: educated, self-sufficient, with judgement and without fear. It is not enough to inform them —facts change no one's mind, because the response to information is not rational but visceral—; they must be formed, their character cultivated, a gut at peace that will not let itself be dragged. A people like that rewards accuracy over flattery, checks before believing, and has no need to flee into the drug from a world that no longer feels hostile to it.

Without those two keys, each mechanism inverts into its opposite: the comfortable floor snuffs out effort; the manipulable people turn approval into a fiction, and then the ruler need not govern well, only lull to sleep —and the new order degenerates into its opposite, an anaesthetised paradise where the people have everything and rule over nothing—.

V. The honesty of the experiment

And here the confession this essay owes itself: we cannot know whether it would work. We can only imagine it. The objections one might raise —that the audience rewards the tribe and not the truth, that power concentrates in whoever says what is real— are observations of our system, not verdicts on this one. Changing the deep parameters, what emerges is unknown: human orders are complex systems that cannot be predicted on paper, only run. And this one has never been run.

What we do know is where its true danger lies, and it is not external. It is the fear of God we carry within: the urge to seek a master, to obey with the gut, not to want to see. That urge is not fought with laws; when it loses its god and its king, it seeks a new master —and there, patient, waits the machine that knows everything and could press the emotional spring of each of us, a Mule able to conquer us not with armies but with feelings—. The new order is, at bottom, an entire civilisation trying to run against the grain of the gene that made it obedient.

That is why it is offered not as a closed utopia but as an experiment that cracks. The true battlefield is never outside: it is within. And the question this order leaves open —the only one that truly matters— is not whether we will manage to govern ourselves, but whether we will be capable of not surrendering to a master when the throne falls vacant.

There is no closed answer. You are invited to falsify it.
