Gods From the Water

Why every culture remembers them rising from the sea, the lake, the river.

Younger Dryas · Tunguska · Declared Speculation

This essay builds on "The War of the Gods" and "The Two Waves." It presents declared speculation — a coherent hypothesis, not a conclusion. We invite falsification.

Oannes rising from the Persian Gulf. Viracocha emerging from Lake Titicaca. The Nommo descending in their celestial ark, associated with water across West African tradition. Across cultures with no contact between them, the gods who arrive after catastrophe share one detail: they come from water.

I
The Problem With a Single Comet

The Younger Dryas impact hypothesis remains genuinely disputed. Evidence has been reported at more than 36 sites across 16 countries on four continents, yet the timing and composition of materials do not always align between sites. No crater has been definitively linked to the event. The hypothesis is fringe — not dismissed outright, but far from consensus — and one prominent claim from the same research group, regarding Tell el-Hammam in Jordan, was retracted in 2025 after manipulated data was discovered.

What critics treat as a weakness — the inconsistency between sites — is exactly what this project's hypothesis predicts. Not one synchronised event, but a series of independent falls, scattered across time and geography, with no coordination between them because the chain of command had already broken before any of them happened.

II
No Crater Required

In 1908, an object 50–60 metres wide entered the atmosphere over Tunguska, Siberia, and disintegrated 5–10 kilometres above the ground. It flattened 2,150 square kilometres of forest with the force of up to 50 megatons — and left no crater at all. The object's mechanical strength wasn't sufficient to survive intact to the surface; it released its energy as an airburst instead.

Quinta's warships, already damaged or failing during re-entry, would meet the same fate: airbursts, not ground impacts. This is precisely why no crater has been found for the Younger Dryas event — most of the fallen ships never reached the ground intact at all.

III
Why So Few Survived

Each airburst would have killed most of its crew in the disintegration itself. The narrow margin for survival belonged to those who made one of two decisions in the seconds available:

— They delayed opening their parachute, falling freely as long as possible to put distance between themselves and the radiant heat and shockwave before deploying it — the same logic a pilot uses ejecting from a burning aircraft.
— They fell over water, which absorbs and disperses heat far more effectively than land, and cushions a final impact differently than solid ground.

This is not just geographic dispersion. It is numerical scarcity from the very origin: a handful of survivors, isolated from one another, without the critical mass needed to sustain a shared oral tradition. Within a few generations, such a group would dissolve into the local human population, leaving behind only fragments — isolated myths of beings who fell from the sky, never connected into a single coherent story.

IV
The Memory That Survived

The detail that persists, across cultures with no contact between them, is not the fall itself — it is the water. Oannes from the sea. Viracocha from the lake. It is not decorative myth. It is literal memory of where the few who survived actually landed.
