The Last Supper

Philological sources and biotechnological reinterpretation

✅ PART I — ACADEMIC CONSENSUS: THE PHILOLOGICAL SOURCES

1. Paul — 1 Corinthians 11:23–25 (~54 A.D.)

The oldest written source, predating all the Gospels. Paul uses the Greek verb φάγετε (phagete) — to eat, to consume. A neutral, non-physical verb with no masticatory connotation.

2. Mark (~70 A.D.) — The Oldest Gospel

Remarkably, for the bread Mark does not include the command "eat" — only "take". The explicit order to eat does not appear. This is significant: the oldest Gospel account is also the most restrained about the physical act.

3. Matthew and Luke (~80–90 A.D.)

Both use φάγετε — to eat — without the masticatory connotation of later Latin. The Greek original remains closer to assimilation than to physical chewing.

4. The Canonical Latin Text — Canon of the Mass

Qui pridie quam pateretur, accepit panem in sanctas ac venerabiles manus suas... fregit, deditque discipulis suis, dicens: Accipite et manducate ex hoc omnes: Hoc est enim Corpus meum.
"Who on the eve of his Passion took bread in his holy and venerable hands... broke it and gave it to his disciples, saying: Take and eat of this, all of you: for this is my Body."
Hic est enim calix Sanguinis mei, novi et aeterni testamenti, mysterium fidei, qui pro vobis et pro multis effundetur in remissionem peccatorum.
"For this is the chalice of my Blood, of the new and eternal testament, the mystery of faith, which will be shed for you and for many for the forgiveness of sins."

5. The Key Philological Finding

The Latin manducare — to chew physically — is a more corporeal and concrete translation than the Greek original phagete. The Latin Church hardened the verb in translation, emphasising physical incorporation.

If we restore the Greek phagete to its fuller meaning — not chew but assimilate, incorporate, make part of yourself — the instruction becomes molecular:

Not consume the form. Incorporate the information. The difference between eating bread and reading a genome.

The instruction to recognise universal kinship is the instruction to recognise the shared genomic origin: all organisms that carry the same base code are, in the strictest molecular sense, kin.
