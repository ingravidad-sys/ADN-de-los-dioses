✅ PART I — THE PHILOLOGICAL KEY: THE VERB THEY CHANGED

The Instruction That Was Hardened

The oldest written source of the Last Supper is Paul’s First Letter to the Corinthians (~54 A.D.), predating all four Gospels. Paul uses the Greek φαγετε (phagete): to eat, to incorporate, to make part of oneself. A verb of assimilation, not mastication. The Gospel of Mark (~70 A.D.), the oldest Gospel, is even more restrained: for the bread it gives no eating command at all — only “take.” Matthew and Luke (~80–90 A.D.) restore phagete — still neutral, still non-physical.

Then the Latin Canon crystallises the tradition — and changes the verb:

Accipite et manducate ex hoc omnes: Hoc est enim Corpus meum.
“Take and chew of this, all of you: for this is my Body.”
Hic est enim calix Sanguinis mei, novi et aeterni testamenti, mysterium fidei, qui pro vobis et pro multis effundetur in remissionem peccatorum.
“For this is the chalice of my Blood, of the new and eternal testament, the mystery of faith, which will be shed for you and for many for the forgiveness of sins.”

The philological finding: “assimilate” is closer to the Greek original than the later Latin. The most technically precise interpretation is also the most ancient: you do not chew a data device. You assimilate its contents.
