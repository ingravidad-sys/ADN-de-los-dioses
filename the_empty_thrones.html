The Empty Thrones

The urge to obey is innate; the master is a vacancy that almost anything can fill. And there is never just one empty seat: there are many.

DNA of the Gods · A declared relectura

In "The Fear-of-God Cluster" an uncomfortable hypothesis was raised: that the disposition to obey, to submit to authority and to belong to a group is not merely culture, but has a measurable, heritable component. If that is true — even in part — one question completes it, and makes it more disquieting still.

If the urge to bend the knee comes as standard, before what do we bend it? And the answer seems to be: before almost anything. The need is fixed; its object is not. The master is a vacancy, a throne that history fills with one occupant after another. And — this is the part usually forgotten — it is not a single throne, but a whole pantheon of them.

The innate and the contingent

Two things are almost always confused and should be kept apart. On one side, the urge: the inclination to defer, to conform and to belong. The large twin studies place the heritability of attitudes toward authority, traditionalism and conformity at around 40–50%. It is not pure culture; there is biology underneath.

On the other side, the object: whom or what the urge is aimed at. And there, nothing is written in the genes. Two classic experiments lay it almost bare. In Stanley Milgram's, ordinary people administered what they believed were painful shocks to a stranger simply because a figure in a lab coat told them to: obedience latched onto whoever occupied the seat of authority. In Henri Tajfel's "minimal group" studies, merely sorting people into two teams by the toss of a coin was enough for them to start favouring their own and distrusting the others. Loyalty needed no reason; a pretext would do.

The urge arrives factory-fitted; the master is a post that almost any candidate can fill — even one chosen on the flip of a coin.

Not one seat: many

Here is the decisive correction. We tend to picture a single throne — God, the king — that is either occupied or left empty. But the urge is not monogamous: it distributes its devotion across several thrones at once, one for each sphere of life. The same person can kneel, in parallel and without feeling any contradiction, before:

  • Politics — the party as a church, the leader as a pastor, the opponent as a heretic.
  • Religion — the original throne, the oldest and most explicit.
  • Sport — the stadium as a cathedral, the colours as a flag, the rivalry as war by proxy.
  • The nation — the homeland, the anthem, the sacralised border.
  • Ideology — the system of ideas that explains everything and admits no doubt.
  • The idol and the brand — the celebrity, the consumer tribe, faith in a logo.

And the most revealing part: the thrones are communicating vessels. When one empties, the devotion does not evaporate — it migrates. Where traditional religion wanes, the fervour does not vanish; it reappears as political militancy, as footballing passion, as nationalism, as the cult of a celebrity. The twentieth century proved it on a grand scale: wherever the altars were torn down, secular religions rose in their place — great party-faiths that demanded orthodoxy, purged heretics and promised paradise on earth — and they did so, lest any partisan reading creep in, at both ends of the spectrum alike. The human material was the same; only the name of the god changed.

The procession of occupants

Seen this way, the history of belief looks less like an evolution and more like a succession of tenants in the same thrones: the spirits and the ancestors, then the gods, then the god-king (the pharaoh, where the sacred and power fuse), then the monarch by divine right and the Church, then the nation and its flag, then the Party and the charismatic leader, then the market and fame. The face on the throne changes; the knee that bends does not.

That is why the famous verdict that "God is dead" was so naïve. An occupant may have died. The throne — the thrones — were not left empty for an instant: there was always something clambering up onto them. In this architecture of the soul, a vacant seat is not a peaceful absence; it is a summons.

The new claimant: the algorithm

And here comes the piece that makes the ancient urge urgent. The candidate climbing onto today's empty thrones is not a god, nor a king, nor a party: it is the algorithm. An authority that presents itself as omniscient, that never sleeps, that seems to know everything about each of us, and that — this is the decisive part — defines what is real: it decides what we see, what is hidden from us, what counts as news and what simply does not exist. Before an arbiter like that, deference is not reasoned: it is felt. We hand it our attention, our judgement and, little by little, our verdict.

What is unsettling is not that an algorithm should occupy one throne, but that it threatens to occupy them all at once: mediating politics, shaping sport and the idol, feeding ideology, replacing the oracle. It would be the first claimant capable of unifying the entire pantheon under a single voice. The oldest instinct of the species finding, at last, its most complete master.

The limits — rigour declared

None of the above licenses genetic determinism, which is how this ground tends to be misused. There is no "obedience gene": what is inherited is diffuse, polygenic and probabilistic — a loaded die, not a script. Heritable does not mean immutable: culture and institutions hugely modulate how the trait is expressed, and societies vary enormously. Caution is also due with the flashy findings: the idea that political orientation shows up in physiological responses to fear (Oxley et al., 2008) failed to replicate in 2020. And the species carries, in equal measure, the opposite vein — curiosity, scepticism, rebellion: within us the submissive and the insubordinate live side by side. This is offered, then, as a hypothesis, not a dogma. You are invited to falsify it.

The question that remains

If the reading is right, the question of each age is not whether we will kneel, but before what. And a harder one still: can we learn to leave a throne empty without rushing to fill it? Or, at least, to aim that urge at something that does not enslave — the law we give ourselves among equals, the reason that consents to be corrected, the other whom we look in the eye — rather than at a master who commands from above?

There is no closed answer here. Only the suspicion that we carry within us a throne — many thrones — waiting for an occupant, and the new and vertiginous responsibility of deciding what we seat on them before something seats itself.

The urge is real. The master is a vacancy. And today, for the first time, there is a machine queuing for every throne at once.
